Someone on the show floor asks a silicone face, "Can you shed tears for me?" A few seconds later one drop runs down the cheek. That is **Yansyn-X2**, from **Yanxi Technology** in Ningbo, at the **2026 Inclusion Conference** in Shanghai. The company calls it the first humanoid that can cry. That is their line, not a lab test we can check.

[Yansyn-X2 tear demo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kpGKallwkuk)

The tear is plumbing plus a listening script. Yanxi describes three steps. Software reads the words and the tone and decides whether a sad or sympathetic reply fits. Parts under a medical-grade silicone skin then pull the face into a frown. A separate microfluidic system pushes artificial tears out at the corners of the eyes, so the drop shows up in the same moment as the expression.

The point, in the company's telling, is not that the machine feels upset. It is that the machine can notice upset language and answer with a matching face. Researchers call that kind of work affective computing: software that reads emotion cues and picks a response. Here the response is a frown and a drop of fluid, triggered when someone asks, or when the script hears a sad turn in the talk.

This copy does not walk. It can turn its head, follow a person in the room, and move its arms. Yanxi says later faces will drop the servo motors for muscle-like actuators, so the expressions change more smoothly and with more variety. There is no public list price, no ship window, and no home trial on the record.

Show floors already use talking faces. A tear duct is a new trick on that same job: look a little more like a person so a guest stays. It does not fold laundry, carry a bag, or cross a room. We are not adding a Yansyn-X2 row. A stationary face with no cart is a demo.

If you want a companion you can start today, **ElliQ** is a US desk unit on a membership and **LOVOT** is a Japan pet you can order. **Unitree G1** is the two-legged kit with a public shop price if the point is a body that actually walks.
