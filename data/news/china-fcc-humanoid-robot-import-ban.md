**China** accused the **United States** of trying to **suppress** Chinese robotics firms after Washington put foreign-made **humanoid** and **quadruped** robots on the **FCC Covered List**. [Hong Kong Free Press](https://hongkongfp.com/2026/07/30/china-accuses-us-of-suppression-over-humanoid-robot-ban/) carried the **AFP** report on **July 30, 2026**. We are filing it the same day for readers watching US buy paths on Chinese platforms.

The **FCC** update landed **July 28**. It adds **foreign-produced advanced robotic devices** to the same security list that already covers certain drones and companies such as **Huawei**. New foreign-made models that walk on two or four legs face a block on fresh **FCC equipment authorization**, unless they win a **Conditional Approval** from the **Department of War**. Models the FCC already authorized, and robots already in US buyers' hands, stay put.

## What Beijing said

Foreign ministry spokesperson **Mao Ning** said China **firmly opposed** the move and argued Washington is **overstretching** national security to hit Chinese companies. Beijing said it will take **necessary measures** to protect Chinese firms' interests, and warned that **protectionism** will not boost US competitiveness. It will hurt American companies and consumers instead.

That framing matters because most humanoids sold into the US today are **imported**, with **China** supplying a large share. AFP notes robots already fill hotels, malls, and factories across China, while US deployments are still mostly **industrial** spaces such as auto plants and warehouses.

## What the rule actually covers

The robotics entry targets **mobile** platforms that mimic human or animal walking: humanoids, robot dogs, and related ground robots that meet the FCC's connectivity tests. **Stationary** industrial arms, many medical robots, and drones under separate rules sit outside this bucket. Foreign-owned brands that **build in the United States** can also stay clear. That is why **Spot** from Boston Dynamics is a different story from a China-built biped seeking a first-time US authorization.

Security agencies argue connected foreign robots can collect data, open cybersecurity holes, or threaten critical infrastructure. Press coverage has pointed to prior Unitree research flaws as one example of that narrative. The list is **forward-looking**: new models need approval; old authorized SKUs can still ship.
